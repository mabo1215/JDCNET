\noindent\textbf{Manuscript:} ``JDCNet Uses Confidence Gated Privileged Modality Distillation for Cost Preserving X Ray Inference''\\
\textbf{Journal:} Discover Artificial Intelligence\\
\textbf{Submission ID:} bf05c1ad-f7f2-44f8-a235-de9eeb659798

We thank the Editor and the Reviewers for their careful assessment. We have
revised the manuscript and respond below to every comment, point by point.
Reviewer comments are reproduced in italics. Section, table, figure, and
equation numbers refer to the revised manuscript (main text and Appendix~A).

The Editor asked us to ensure that results are accurately reported, that
overstated conclusions are rewritten, and that limitations are fully
explained. Reviewer~6 raised closely related points about cross-validation,
configuration selection, and statistical reporting. We responded in three steps.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item We retrieved the complete per-run records of the original experiments from the original computing host and re-audited every number in the manuscript against them and against the training code. The audit led to substantive corrections, listed openly in Section~\ref{sec:corrections} below.
  \item We wrote an analysis plan, committed it to the code repository, and then ran a supplementary experiment on the original splits: 240 batch-matched supervised restarts, per-patient predictions for every checkpoint, and teacher-calibration diagnostics. This experiment directly addresses selection optimism, the training budget of the baseline, patient-level uncertainty, and the external test.
  \item We rewrote the manuscript around the resulting evidence. No individual JDCNet configuration is significant after multiplicity adjustment. However, the training budget is unlikely to explain the results. When both methods select one of 16 runs per cell on the validation partition, JDCNet retains an advantage of $+0.037$ balanced accuracy (BA): 95\% CI $[+0.016,+0.058]$ at the cell level and $[+0.007,+0.068]$ at the patient level. All models fail on an external set, and the conclusions are bounded accordingly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Corrections to previously reported results}
\label{sec:corrections}

We regret that the previous version contained statements and numbers that are
not supported by our records or by the training code. Each one has been
corrected or removed.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Pre-registration claim.} The previous version stated that the full JDCNet grid had been registered on a 226-patient pilot before the 510-patient cohort was unblinded. This was incorrect. The decision rule (mean $\Delta\mathrm{BA}\geq+0.03$ and lower CI bound $>0$) was written in the analysis plan before any JDCNet run. The configurations, however, were explored in three stages on the 510-patient cohort, and that cohort had already been used for other comparisons. The two configurations that met the rule came from extension stages added after an initial eight-configuration grid had met it in none of its configurations. Section~3.7 now describes this chronology. Section~4.7 reports a nested comparison planned before it was run.
  \item \textbf{Absolute performance table (Table~5).} The previous values were inconsistent with the recorded results. Every BA was about $0.021$ too low; for example, the supervised baseline BA is $0.625$, not $0.604$. The AUC, sensitivity, and specificity values were also inconsistent. Table~5 is now computed from the per-run records.
  \item \textbf{``Sensitivity gain'' interpretation.} JDCNet's gains came through specificity, not sensitivity. The mean specificity change is positive for all 16 configurations, and the mean sensitivity change is negative for 12 (Table~A1).
  \item \textbf{Fold/seed win counts.} Eight of the 16 ``Pos./15'' entries in the sweep table were wrong. They are corrected from the records (Table~4, now reported as positive/tied/negative).
  \item \textbf{BiomedCLIP control.} This control was evaluated on a 226-patient balanced subset, not on the 510-patient cohort. It is now reported with its correct cohort (Section~4.5).
  \item \textbf{Comparators described as run ``under the same splits''.} Attention transfer, feature hints, and the module-augmented pilot were run only in an early small-cohort stage. Section~3.5 now states the cohort for every comparator.
  \item \textbf{Comparator tuning budget.} On the 510-patient cohort, each logit-KD comparator was run with a single setting per teacher view ($T{=}4$, $\alpha{=}0.6$), not the $4\times4$ grid previously stated. The previously reported same-modality distillation check has no record on any of our computing hosts and has been removed.
  \item \textbf{Gate-coverage and teacher-calibration table.} The previous table included thresholds and teacher views for which no JDCNet runs exist, and its values could not be traced to any computation. The aggregate gate-behavior figure based on it has also been removed. We recomputed the diagnostics from CT views re-rendered with the original code (Table~6). The recomputed values differ substantially. For example, the gate retains 84--95\% of training samples, not the 63--74\% stated before, and the held-out teacher ECE is $0.17$--$0.19$.
  \item \textbf{Lightweight-backbone results (MobileNetV2, EfficientNet-B0).} No runs or code for these results exist on any of our computing hosts. They have been removed, and lightweight students are listed as future work. We apologize to Reviewer~3 of the previous round, to whom these results were presented as a response.
  \item \textbf{Patient-level bootstrap table.} The original evaluation pipeline stored only aggregate metrics, so the previously reported patient-level intervals could not have been computed as described. We have now computed patient-level bootstrap intervals from newly generated per-patient predictions (Tables~2 and~A6).
  \item \textbf{Cross-source non-COVID control table.} This control used checkpoints from the early 19-patient study, not models trained ``as in the main BIMCV protocol'', and its values have no record. It has been removed.
  \item \textbf{Deployment latency table.} The previous CPU, GPU, and edge (Jetson) timings and the $1.18\times$ training overhead could not be traced to a measurement. We re-measured inference latency and memory with a released script and report them as illustrative (Table~9). The robust conclusion is structural: JDCNet and the supervised baseline share one deployed computation graph. The previous figure caption that quoted $0.094$\,M parameters for the ResNet-18 deployment path was wrong, and the figure has been redrawn.
  \item \textbf{Cohort construction rules.} The previous appendix described a $\pm14$-day pairing window, an earliest-pair rule, hashed-identifier splitting, and per-fold normalization statistics. None of these is implemented in the cohort-construction code. Appendix~A.3 and Section~4.1 now describe the rules actually used, and two consequences are stated as limitations: pairs are same-patient but not necessarily same-visit, and the two classes come from two BIMCV releases.
  \item \textbf{Implementation details.} The following are corrected in Section~4.2:
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item CT teachers were trained within every fold and seed, not ``pre-trained once''.
    \item Checkpoints were selected on a validation fold.
    \item Batch sizes differed between arms.
    \item The initial 120-run JDCNet stage took about 32 minutes on two GPUs, not ``6 hours''.
    \item The 3-slice view stacks three different slices.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{External test.} The previously reported external evaluation used students trained on a smaller BIMCV subset, not the 510-patient models. The revised Section~4.10 evaluates the actual 510-patient models and reaches the same conclusion.
  \item \textbf{Gate-temperature rerun.} This rerun was performed on a different host, reports validation-partition BA, and partly used approximated CT views. It is now in Appendix~A.8 with these caveats and is superseded by the supplementary experiment.
\end{enumerate}

All cell-level statistics in the revised manuscript are computed by released scripts from
the released per-run records. The patient-level, external, and teacher analyses
additionally use per-image predictions, which are indexed by BIMCV and MIDRC
identifiers and are therefore available from the corresponding author on
request rather than in the public repository.

\section*{Reviewer 1}

\begin{rcomment}
1. Why is the evaluation only done on COVID-19 diagnosis and can this approach be extended to multi-class (multi-disease) problems?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} The study requires CT and chest radiographs of the
\emph{same} patients with labels. The only public cohort of this kind that was
available to us is the BIMCV-COVID19 collection, which defines a binary
COVID-19 versus non-COVID task. We now state this reason explicitly and treat
the binary task as a limitation (Section~4.12, ``Task scope'').

Methodologically, the JDCNet objective extends directly to $K$ mutually
exclusive classes: the gate uses $c_i=\max_k p^{ct}_{i,k}$, and the hard or
soft-KL auxiliary target is defined over all $K$ classes. A new paragraph
(Section~3.4, ``Extension beyond two classes'') explains two points. First,
class-conditional thresholds may be needed for imbalanced label spaces, because
the temperature--threshold equivalence of Eq.~(4) no longer holds exactly for
$K>2$. Second, for multi-label chest radiograph findings the natural analog is
a per-finding sigmoid teacher with a per-finding mask. We cite recent
multi-label chest-radiograph work (CXR-LT, CheXzero) as the relevant setting.
No multi-disease same-patient CT--X-ray cohort was available to us, so the
paper states that this extension is untested.
\where{Section~3.4 (``Extension beyond two classes''); Section~4.12 (``Task scope''); Section~2.2.}

\begin{rcomment}
2. How do you justify the performance drop of the model (BA approx 0.49) on the external data (MIDRC) and what is your solution to deal with domain changes?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We do not treat the external result as something to justify
away. In the revision we evaluate the actual 510-patient models with per-image
predictions on the 126 external radiographs (Section~4.10, Table~10). Every
model performs at chance, the supervised students as well as JDCNet: BA
$0.49$--$0.51$, AUC $0.49$--$0.51$, and all 95\% CIs include $0.5$. Paired
DeLong tests show no difference between JDCNet and supervised students, for
example $\Delta\mathrm{AUC}=-0.032$, CI $[-0.087,+0.023]$. Because AUC is also
at chance, the failure is a loss of ranking signal, not a threshold offset.

We list possible causes without claiming to have identified one:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Acquisition differences.} We found a concrete candidate: in the training cohort, computed radiography makes up 62\% of the positive and 45\% of the negative radiographs ($p=0.002$), whereas 85\% of the external radiographs are digital radiography. Detector-related appearance is therefore associated with the training label but not with the external one.
  \item label-definition differences;
  \item a release-level source confound in the training data;
  \item prevalence and population differences;
  \item preprocessing differences.
\end{itemize}
The remedies we describe include multi-center training with site-held-out
evaluation, single-domain generalization such as spectral adversarial
augmentation (Zhang et al., AAAI 2023), acquisition-noise augmentation,
harmonization or stratification of detector types, domain adaptation, and
site-specific threshold recalibration. None of these remedies is evaluated
here.
\where{Section~4.10 and Table~10; Section~4.12 (``Data and generalization'').}

\begin{rcomment}
3. Given the drastic changes in performance by moving the $\zeta_{\mathrm{gate}}$ and $\lambda$ parameters, how likely is overfitting on this dataset?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We now answer this quantitatively (Section~4.4, ``Sensitivity to $\tau_{\mathrm{gate}}$ and $\lambda$, and risk of overfitting''; Section~4.7).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{The changes across $\tau$ and $\lambda$ are within the noise.} Moving $\tau_{\mathrm{gate}}$ from 0.70 to 0.80 with the other settings fixed changes $\Delta\mathrm{BA}$ by at most $0.021$. The standard deviation of the 16 configuration means ($0.011$) is smaller than their typical standard error ($0.014$). For comparison, 16 supervised restarts that differed only in the random seed varied with a within-cell standard deviation of $0.041$ BA. Run-to-run variability, not $\tau$ or $\lambda$, therefore dominates the spread of individual runs.
  \item \emph{Model-level overfitting to the test folds is limited.} Checkpoints were selected on a separate validation fold, and CT teachers were trained within each fold.
  \item \emph{Selection overfitting is real, and we quantified and controlled it.}
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item With directly computed paired $t$-tests, the two best configurations have $p=0.014$ and $0.028$; after Holm adjustment over 16 configurations these become $0.22$ and $0.42$. No single configuration is significant after adjustment.
    \item In a pre-registered nested comparison, both JDCNet and supervised training selected one of 16 runs per cell on the validation partition, so no test data were used for selection. JDCNet retained an advantage of $+0.037$ BA (CI $[+0.016,+0.058]$; patient-level $[+0.007,+0.068]$).
    \item Validation-based selection chose 11 different configurations across the 15 cells, so the advantage belongs to the configuration family rather than to one tuned setting.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\where{Sections~3.7, 4.4, and 4.7; Tables~2 and 4; Appendix Tables~A1 and A6.}

\begin{rcomment}
5. How much valuable uncertainty information is lost by replacing the teacher's soft distribution with hard argmax values?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We answer this analytically and empirically (Section~3.4, ``Information discarded by the hard target''; Section~4.4).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Analytically.} For two classes the teacher's distribution is fully described by its confidence $c_i$, and the non-target-class term of decoupled KD vanishes. For an admitted sample ($c_i>\tau$), replacing the distribution by its argmax label discards only the confidence magnitude above the threshold. The divergence between the one-hot target and the teacher distribution is below $0.36$ nats at $\tau=0.70$ and below $0.22$ nats at $\tau=0.80$; the discarded teacher entropy is below $0.88$ and $0.72$ bits. Below-threshold samples are excluded by either variant, so the gate itself is where the teacher's uncertainty is used.
  \item \emph{Empirically.} At matched settings, the soft-KL target minus the hard target gives $+0.028$, $+0.001$, $-0.003$, and $+0.010$ BA (average $+0.009$), which is within the sampling uncertainty.
  \item \emph{Mechanistically.} The new teacher diagnostics (Table~6) explain why so little is lost. On its own training patients, where the gate operates, the teacher is 94--96\% accurate, and about 97\% of admitted argmax targets equal the ground-truth label. The auxiliary target therefore acts mainly as CT-informed sample weighting, and the soft-KL variant additionally conveys the teacher's confidence.
\end{itemize}
\where{Section~3.4; Section~4.4 and Table~6; Table~4.}

\begin{rcomment}
6. Why weren't corrective techniques such as Temperature Scaling used to address possible overconfidence in the teacher model before applying the confidence gate?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We added an analysis and the corresponding measurements.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Temperature scaling of the gate is a change of threshold} (Section~3.4, Eq.~(4)). For a two-class teacher with logit margin $z_i$, gating the temperature-scaled confidence $\sigma(|z_i|/T_{\mathrm{g}})$ at $\tau$ is identical to gating the raw confidence at $\tau'=\sigma(T_{\mathrm{g}}\,\mathrm{logit}(\tau))$, and the argmax label is unchanged. Fitting a calibration temperature (Guo et al., 2017) therefore re-indexes $\tau$ rather than adding a new safeguard.
  \item \emph{Measured calibration} (Table~6). On held-out patients the teachers are over-confident: validation and test ECE are $0.17$--$0.19$, and the validation-fitted temperature is $1.7$. With that temperature, the effective raw threshold of the mid-slice configuration would move from $0.80$ to $0.92$, and that of the 3-slice configuration from $0.70$ to $0.81$. The value $0.81$ is close to the explored value $0.80$, whereas $0.92$ lies above both explored values ($0.70$ and $0.80$). On the training partition, where the gate actually operates, the teachers are nearly calibrated (ECE $0.02$--$0.03$), because they were fitted to those patients. Held-out calibration therefore does not describe the gate's operating regime.
  \item \emph{What temperature scaling cannot do.} It cannot correct the ranking of teacher predictions: a confidently wrong prediction remains confidently wrong (Table~7, Case~B). Cross-fitted teachers, class-conditional thresholds, and calibration beyond a single temperature are listed as open refinements (Section~4.12, ``Mechanism'').
\end{itemize}
\where{Section~3.4 (Eq.~(4)); Section~4.4 and Table~6; Table~7; Section~4.12; Appendix~A.8.}

The manuscript states that each CT teacher view was ``pre-trained once'' and that the frozen checkpoint was reused across student runs. \textbf{Response.} Thank you for catching this ambiguity. The teachers were
trained \emph{within each fold}, so no held-out test patient was ever seen by a
teacher. The procedure is as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item For every fold and seed, a separate CT teacher for each view was trained only on that fold's three training folds, about 306 patients.
  \item Its checkpoint was selected on the fold's validation partition.
  \item It was then frozen and used only for the students of the same fold and seed.
\end{itemize}
This gives 15 teachers per view (five folds $\times$ three seeds). The phrase
``pre-trained once'' was inaccurate and has been removed. Section~4.2,

explicitly; in each fold, fold $k$ is the test partition and fold $(k{+}1) \bmod 5$ the
validation partition.

One consequence of within-fold training is now analyzed explicitly
(Section~4.4, Table~6). Because each teacher supervises the training patients
on which it was itself fitted, its confidence on those patients is high, and
