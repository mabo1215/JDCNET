\noindent\textbf{Manuscript:} ``JDCNet Uses Confidence Gated Privileged Modality Distillation for Cost Preserving X Ray Inference''\\
\textbf{Journal:} Discover Artificial Intelligence\\
\textbf{Submission ID:} bf05c1ad-f7f2-44f8-a235-de9eeb659798

We thank the Editor and the Reviewers for their careful assessment. We have
revised the manuscript and respond below to every comment, point by point.
Reviewer comments are reproduced in italics. Section, table, figure, and
equation numbers refer to the revised manuscript (main text and Appendix~A).

The Editor asked us to ensure that results are accurately reported, that
overstated conclusions are rewritten, and that limitations are fully
explained. Reviewer~6 raised closely related points about cross-validation,
configuration selection, and statistical reporting. In response we re-audited
every number in the manuscript against the archived experiment records and the
analysis scripts. This audit led to substantive corrections, which we list
openly in Section~\ref{sec:corrections} before the point-by-point responses.
The central consequence is that the main JDCNet result is now presented as
exploratory, selection-adjusted evidence of a small effect rather than as a
validated gain. We believe this is the accurate reading of our data, and it
directly addresses the concerns of Reviewer~6 and the Editor.

\section*{Summary of principal changes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Selection history and selection-adjusted inference (Sections~3.7 and~4.4; Tables~2, 4, A1).} The manuscript now states exactly how the 16 JDCNet configurations were explored, in three stages on the same 510-patient cohort. It reports directly computed per-configuration $p$-values, Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment, unselected averages, and a simulation of selection inflation. The abstract, contributions, discussion, and conclusion are rewritten accordingly.
  \item \textbf{Training-budget confound disclosed (Sections~3.7, 4.7, 4.12).} The supervised baseline was trained with batch size 512 and JDCNet with batch size 128. Batch-matched reruns are reported, and their mixed results are stated.
  \item \textbf{Corrected absolute metrics, comparator scope, cohort description, and implementation details} (Table~5, Sections~3.5, 4.1, 4.2, 4.5, Appendix~A.3).
  \item \textbf{External stress test with patient-level confidence intervals and corrected provenance} (Section~4.10, Table~9).
  \item \textbf{New analysis of temperature scaling and hard targets} (Section~3.4, Eq.~(4)): for a two-class teacher, temperature scaling of the gate is equivalent to a change of threshold. We also bound the information that the argmax target discards.
  \item \textbf{Moderated clinical and cost statements} (Sections~4.9 and~5, Table~10). Terminology is updated: ``CT-only reference'' replaces ``teacher upper bound'', and Macro-F1 is defined.
  \item \textbf{Literature updated} with 19 references from 2022--2026, including the work suggested by Reviewer~6 and all six works suggested by Reviewer~1.
  \item \textbf{Language and presentation.} Duplicated text is removed, spelling is unified to US English, acronyms are defined on first use, and the mechanism figure is redrawn (Fig.~1).
\end{itemize}

\section{Corrections to previously reported results}
\label{sec:corrections}

We regret that the previous version contained statements and numbers that are
not supported by our archived records. We have corrected or removed each of
them, as follows.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Pre-registration claim.} The previous version stated that the full JDCNet grid had been registered on a 226-patient pilot before the 510-patient cohort was unblinded. This was incorrect. The decision rule (mean $\Delta\mathrm{BA}\geq+0.03$ and lower CI bound $>0$) was written in the analysis plan before any JDCNet run. The configurations themselves, however, were explored in three stages on the 510-patient cohort, and that cohort had already been used for other comparisons. The two configurations that met the rule came from extension stages added after an initial eight-configuration grid met it in none of its configurations. Section~3.7 now describes this chronology, and the results are re-interpreted with selection-adjusted inference (Section~4.4).
  \item \textbf{Absolute performance table (now Table~5).} The previous absolute values were inconsistent with the recorded test-fold results. Every balanced accuracy (BA) was about $0.021$ too low; for example, the supervised baseline BA is $0.625$, not $0.604$. The supervised baseline's sensitivity and specificity were also inconsistent with the records. Table~5 now reports only values that can be recomputed from the records. Where only paired summaries were retained, this is marked.
  \item \textbf{``Sensitivity gain'' interpretation.} In the eight configurations whose per-fold records are archived, the JDCNet difference came mainly through specificity, not sensitivity (Section~4.4; Table~A1). The statement that the gain reflected higher sensitivity has been withdrawn.
  \item \textbf{Fold/seed win counts.} Eight of the 16 ``Pos./15'' entries in the sweep table were wrong. They are corrected from the recorded counts (Table~4, now reported as positive/tied/negative).
  \item \textbf{BiomedCLIP control.} This control was evaluated on a 226-patient balanced subset, not on the 510-patient cohort as previously implied. It is now reported with its correct cohort (Section~4.5).
  \item \textbf{Comparators described as run ``under the same splits''.} Attention transfer, feature hints, and the module-augmented pilot were run only in an early small-cohort stage. Section~3.5 now states the cohort for every comparator.
  \item \textbf{Comparator tuning budget.} On the 510-patient cohort, each logit-KD comparator was run with a single setting per teacher view, not the $4\times4$ grid previously stated. A same-modality distillation ``sanity check'' reported previously has no record and has been removed.
  \item \textbf{Gate-coverage and teacher-calibration table, and the aggregate gate-behavior figure.} These included thresholds ($\tau=0.60$, $0.85$) and teacher views (mean projection, DRR) for which no JDCNet runs exist. The coverage and calibration statistics could not be traced to any analysis record. We removed the table, the figure, and the text that relied on them. The four case-level gate examples (Table~6) are retained because they are reproducible from the archived output.
  \item \textbf{Lightweight-backbone results (MobileNetV2, EfficientNet-B0).} No runs or code for these results exist in our records. They have been removed, and lightweight students are now listed as future work. We apologize to Reviewer~3 of the previous round, to whom these results were presented as a response.
  \item \textbf{Patient-level bootstrap table.} The evaluation pipeline did not store per-patient predictions, so the previously reported patient-level bootstrap intervals could not have been computed as described. The table has been removed. Section~3.7 now states that fold/seed-level intervals understate patient-level uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Cross-source non-COVID control table.} This control used checkpoints from the early 19-patient study, not models trained ``as in the main BIMCV protocol'', and its values have no retained record. It has been removed.
  \item \textbf{Deployment latency table.} The previous CPU, GPU, and edge (Jetson) timings and the $1.18\times$ training overhead could not be traced to a measurement record. We re-measured inference latency and memory with a released script on the hardware available to us, and report those values as illustrative (Table~8). The robust conclusion is structural: JDCNet and the supervised baseline share one deployed computation graph. The training overhead is now stated analytically, as one extra frozen-teacher forward pass per sample. The previous figure caption that quoted $0.094$\,M parameters for the ResNet-18 deployment path was wrong, and the figure has been redrawn.
  \item \textbf{Cohort construction rules.} The previous appendix described a $\pm14$-day pairing window, an earliest-pair rule, hashed patient-identifier splitting, and per-fold normalization statistics. None of these is implemented in the cohort-construction code. Appendix~A.3 and Section~4.1 now describe the rules actually used. Every patient with a retrievable CT volume and chest radiograph is included, without a date window; one radiograph and one CT volume are used per patient; folds are assigned by seeded random permutation within class; and no dataset-level normalization is applied. Two consequences are stated as limitations: pairs are same-patient but not necessarily same-visit, and the two classes come from two BIMCV releases.
  \item \textbf{Implementation details.} The CT teachers were trained within every fold and seed, not ``pre-trained once''. Checkpoints were selected on a validation fold. Batch sizes differed between arms. The initial 120-run JDCNet stage took about 32 minutes on two GPUs, not ``6 hours''. The 3-slice view stacks three different slices rather than replicating one slice. All of these are corrected in Section~4.2.
  \item \textbf{External stress test.} The external evaluation used students from batch-matched cross-validation runs on a class-balanced 216--228-patient BIMCV subset, not the 510-patient models as previously stated. Section~4.10 now says so and adds confidence intervals.
  \item \textbf{Gate-temperature rerun (now Appendix~A.8).} This rerun reports validation-partition BA, on a separate 466-patient split, with one seed. We now state this, report the earlier non-reproducing rerun on the 216-patient subset, and remove the earlier claim that the subset result was ``a small-cohort artefact''.
\end{enumerate}

The statistics in the revised manuscript are regenerated from the archived
records by scripts released with the code. Quantities whose per-run records
were not retained are reported only at the level of the retained summaries,
and the corresponding table cells are marked.

\section*{Reviewer 1}

\begin{rcomment}
1. Why is the evaluation only done on COVID-19 diagnosis and can this approach be extended to multi-class (multi-disease) problems?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} The study requires CT and chest radiographs of the
\emph{same} patients with labels. The only public cohort of this kind that was
available to us is the BIMCV-COVID19 collection, which defines a binary
COVID-19 versus non-COVID task. We now state this reason explicitly and treat
the binary task as a limitation (Section~4.12, ``Task scope'').

Methodologically, the JDCNet objective extends directly to $K$ mutually
exclusive classes. The gate uses $c_i=\max_k p^{ct}_{i,k}$, and the hard or
soft-KL auxiliary target is defined over all $K$ classes. A new paragraph
(Section~3.4, ``Extension beyond two classes'') explains two points. First,
class-conditional thresholds may be needed for imbalanced label spaces, because
the temperature--threshold equivalence of Eq.~(4) no longer holds exactly for
$K>2$. Second, for multi-label chest radiograph findings the natural analogue
is a per-finding sigmoid teacher with a per-finding mask. We cite recent
multi-label chest-radiograph work (CXR-LT, CheXzero) as the relevant setting.
We do not report multi-class experiments, because no multi-disease
same-patient CT--X-ray cohort was available. The paper states that such an
extension is untested.
\where{Section~3.4 (``Extension beyond two classes''); Section~4.12 (``Task scope''); Section~2.2.}

\begin{rcomment}
2. How do you justify the performance drop of the model (BA approx 0.49) on the external data (MIDRC) and what is your solution to deal with domain changes?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We do not treat the external result as something to
justify away. Every student performs at chance on the external set, the
supervised students as well as the JDCNet students, and AUC is also at chance.
The failure is therefore an absence of transferable ranking signal, not a
threshold offset. In the revision we:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item report image-level 95\% CIs for BA and AUC (Table~9). Every interval includes $0.5$, and the BA differences between JDCNet and the supervised students amount to fewer than one radiograph out of 126;
  \item correct the provenance. The evaluated students came from the batch-matched cross-validation runs on a balanced BIMCV subset, not from the 510-patient models;
  \item list possible causes without claiming to have identified one: acquisition and scanner differences; label-definition differences; a source confound in training, because BIMCV positives and negatives come from two releases; prevalence and population differences; and preprocessing differences (Section~4.10);
  \item describe remedies, none of which is evaluated here (Section~4.12). These are multi-center training on paired cohorts with site-held-out evaluation, single-domain generalization methods such as spectral adversarial augmentation (Zhang et al., AAAI 2023), acquisition-noise augmentation, unsupervised domain adaptation, and site-specific threshold recalibration.
\end{itemize}
We also state the implication directly. The CT supervision did not confer
external robustness to these students, and robustness must be established on
independent data.
\where{Section~4.10 and Table~9; Section~4.12 (``Data and generalization'').}

\begin{rcomment}
3. Given the drastic changes in performance by moving the $\zeta_{\mathrm{gate}}$ and $\lambda$ parameters, how likely is overfitting on this dataset?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} This question goes to the heart of the revision, and we
now answer it quantitatively (Section~4.4, ``Sensitivity to $\tau_{\mathrm{gate}}$
and $\lambda$, and risk of overfitting'').
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{The changes are within sampling noise.} Holding the other choices fixed, moving $\tau_{\mathrm{gate}}$ from 0.70 to 0.80 changes $\Delta\mathrm{BA}$ by at most $0.021$. The standard deviation of the 16 configuration means ($0.011$) is smaller than their typical standard error ($0.014$). It is also smaller than the approximate standard error of a difference between two configurations ($0.016$). The apparent sensitivity is therefore compatible with a single common effect.
  \item \emph{Model-level overfitting to the test folds is limited.} Checkpoints were selected on a separate validation fold, and CT teachers were trained within each fold.
  \item \emph{Selection overfitting is real, and we now quantify it.} $\tau_{\mathrm{gate}}$, $\lambda$, and the target type were chosen after inspecting test-fold results on the same 510 patients. After Holm adjustment over 16 configurations, the two best configurations have $p=0.051$ and $p=0.17$. In a simulation where all configurations share the grid-average effect ($+0.020$), the expected best estimate is $+0.040$, and at least two configurations meet the decision rule in 66\% of sweeps. The two best configurations are therefore not distinguishable from the rest, and their estimates are likely optimistic.
\end{itemize}
We conclude that the selected configurations are hypotheses for a nested or
independent-cohort confirmation, and the paper now says so.
\where{Section~4.4; Section~3.7 (``Analysis chronology and selection'', ``Selection simulation''); Table~4; Section~4.12.}

\begin{rcomment}
5. How much valuable uncertainty information is lost by replacing the teacher's soft distribution with hard argmax values?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We now answer this both analytically and empirically
(Section~3.4, ``Information discarded by the hard target''; Section~4.4,
``Hard versus soft-KL target'').
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Analytically.} For two classes, the teacher's distribution is fully described by its confidence $c_i$, and the non-target-class term of decoupled KD vanishes. For an admitted sample ($c_i>\tau$), replacing the distribution by its argmax label discards only the confidence magnitude above the threshold. The divergence between the one-hot target and the teacher distribution is $-\ln c_i<-\ln\tau$, which is below $0.36$ nats at $\tau=0.70$ and below $0.22$ nats at $\tau=0.80$. The discarded teacher entropy is below $0.88$ and $0.72$ bits, respectively. Uncertainty below the threshold is not passed to the student by either variant, because those samples are excluded by the gate. In this sense the gate itself is where the teacher's uncertainty is used.
  \item \emph{Empirically.} At matched teacher view, $\tau$, and $\lambda=1.0$, the soft-KL target minus the hard target gives $+0.028$, $+0.001$, $-0.003$, and $+0.010$ BA, with an average of $+0.009$. Retaining the confidence magnitude may help slightly, but the differences lie within the sampling uncertainty. This is consistent with the small amount of information the hard target discards in the binary setting.
\end{itemize}
\where{Section~3.4; Section~4.4; Table~4.}

\begin{rcomment}
6. Why weren't corrective techniques such as Temperature Scaling used to address possible overconfidence in the teacher model before applying the confidence gate?
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} We added an analysis showing why, in this setting,
temperature scaling before a hard gate is equivalent to a threshold change
(Section~3.4, Eq.~(4)). For a two-class teacher with logit margin $z_i$,
gating the temperature-scaled confidence $\sigma(|z_i|/T_{\mathrm{g}})$ at $\tau$ is
identical to gating the raw confidence at
$\tau'=\sigma(T_{\mathrm{g}}\,\mathrm{logit}(\tau))$. The argmax label is unchanged.
Fitting a calibration temperature on validation data (Guo et al., 2017)
would therefore re-index $\tau$ rather than add a new safeguard. The
batch-matched rerun with gate temperatures $T_{\mathrm{g}}\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}$ is exactly
such a re-indexing: it corresponds to raw thresholds $\{0.60,0.70,0.84\}$
and $\{0.67,0.80,0.94\}$ for the two configurations. Within each
configuration, BA changed by at most $0.025$ (Appendix~A.8, Table~A5).

Temperature scaling also cannot correct the \emph{ranking} of teacher
predictions. A confidently wrong prediction remains confidently wrong;
Table~6, Case~B, shows such a prediction admitted by the gate. Remedies that
would go beyond threshold re-indexing are class-conditional thresholds, vector
scaling, and gates that use teacher--label agreement. These are listed as open
questions (Section~4.12, ``Calibration and gate design''). We also note that
the gate-temperature rerun uses validation-partition metrics and a single
seed, so it is not confirmatory.
\where{Section~3.4 (Eq.~(4)); Appendix~A.8 and Table~A5; Table~6; Section~4.12.}

\section*{Reviewer 6}

\begin{rcomment}
(1) Clarify whether CT teachers were trained separately within each cross-validation fold. The manuscript states that each CT teacher view was ``pre-trained once'' and that the frozen checkpoint was reused across student runs. It is unclear whether this means one teacher was trained within each training fold and reused only across seeds and student configurations belonging to that fold, or one teacher was trained on the complete 510-patient cohort and then used in all five cross-validation folds.
\end{rcomment}

\textbf{Response.} Thank you for catching this ambiguity. The teachers were
trained \emph{within each fold}, so no held-out test patient was ever seen by
a teacher. For every fold and seed, a separate CT teacher for each view was
trained only on that fold's three training folds, about 306 patients. Its
checkpoint was selected on the fold's validation partition, and it was then
frozen and used only for the students of the same fold and seed. This gives 15
teachers per view (five folds $\times$ three seeds). The JDCNet runs reuse
the teachers trained for the CT-only reference rows of the same fold and seed.
The phrase ``pre-trained once'' was inaccurate and has been removed. Section~4.2
(``CT teachers are trained within each fold''), Algorithm~1, Fig.~1, and
Appendix~A.3, rule~(vi), now state the procedure explicitly. In each fold,
fold $k$ is the test partition and fold $k{+}1$ the validation partition.
\where{Section~4.2; Algorithm~1; Fig.~1; Appendix~A.3.}

\begin{rcomment}
(2) The manuscript evaluates 16 JDCNet configurations on the same 510-patient cohort and designates the two configurations that satisfy the decision gate as validated operating points. The same cross-validation predictions are therefore used both to select the configurations and to estimate their performance. The Benjamini--Hochberg procedure may address one aspect of multiple hypothesis testing, but it does not remove the optimism introduced by selecting configurations and reporting their cross-validated performance on the same patients. \textbf{Response.} We agree with every part of this comment. Our re-audit
also showed that the configuration search was more adaptive than the previous
version described. The configurations were explored in three stages on the
same cohort, and the two rule-meeting configurations came from extension
stages added after the initial grid met the rule in none of its configurations
(Section~\ref{sec:corrections}, item~1). We have therefore withdrawn the
designation ``validated operating points'' and revised the analysis as
follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Chronology disclosed (Section~3.7, ``Analysis chronology and selection'').} The decision rule preceded the JDCNet runs, but the configurations did not. Table~4 gives the stage of every configuration.
  \item \emph{Directly calculated $p$-values (Table~4, Table~A1).} For all 16 configurations we report an exact sign test computed directly from the recorded fold/seed win counts, and a Wald-type $p$-value recovered from the recorded bootstrap interval, with its derivation stated. For the eight configurations whose per-cell records are archived, we also report paired $t$-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, and bootstrap $p$-values computed directly from the 15 paired cells. The $t$-test values (and seven of the eight Wilcoxon values) are somewhat larger than the Wald-type values, so the latter are, if anything, optimistic.
  \item \emph{Multiplicity adjustment.} The two selected configurations have unadjusted Wald-type $p=0.003$ and $0.011$. Holm adjustment gives $0.051$ and $0.17$, Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment gives $0.051$ and $0.092$, and sign tests give $0.30$ and $0.18$ unadjusted.
  \item \emph{Unselected estimates.} The average over all 16 configurations is $+0.020$, with 15 of 16 positive. The per-cell average over the eight initially planned configurations involves no configuration selection; it is $+0.016$ (95\% CI $[-0.000,+0.035]$, paired $t$-test $p=0.11$).
  \item \emph{Selection optimism quantified.} A simulation with the recorded standard errors and the observed between-configuration correlation ($0.34$) shows the following. If all configurations shared the grid-average effect, the expected best estimate would be $+0.040$, and two configurations would meet the rule in 66\% of sweeps. If all true effects were zero, two would meet it in 5.1\% of sweeps. The best two configurations are therefore not identified as better than the others, and their estimates are likely optimistic.
  \item \emph{A further confound disclosed.} The supervised baseline was trained with batch size 512 and JDCNet with 128, so JDCNet received three times as many optimizer updates. Batch-matched reruns are mixed (Section~4.7).
\end{itemize}
We agree that the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure does not remove selection
optimism. It is now presented only as one of several adjustments, and the text
says explicitly that the selected estimates are exploratory. Nested
cross-validation of the whole sweep was not feasible within the revision
period. We therefore recommend, and describe, a confirmatory design (Section~4.12):
one fixed configuration, a batch-matched baseline, and an untouched or
independent cohort. The abstract, contributions, discussion, and conclusion
now reflect this evidence level.
